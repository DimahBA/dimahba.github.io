Adaptive AR pathfinding for Parkinson's disease

A Meta Quest 3 system that lets people with Parkinson's point at where they want to go, then lays a walking path across their home's floor, avoiding every obstacle and furniture.

Project Summary

01 — Problem

Freezing of gait is a common problem for people with Parkinson's disease. It causes people to suddenly freeze while walking, often in doorways or narrow spaces, which can lead to falls and injuries.

02 — Known

External cueing, such as lines on the floor or auditory beats, can help people with Parkinson's initiate movement and overcome freezing episodes.

03 — Gap

Existing AR systems for cueing require the route to be pre-authored by a researcher, which does not allow patients to choose their own destinations.

04 — Solution

Patients can point at a destination, and the system maps the room, solves a safe route around the furniture, and lays cues on the floor.

01 — The problem

Parkinson's affects gait.

And then it stops it.

Freezing of gait is a common symptom of Parkinson's disease, it can lead to falls and injuries.

02 — Why a cue works

Planning movement

The basal ganglia are responsible for coordinating movement. they recieve signals from the brain about what movement is intended, and then send signals to the muscles to execute that movement.

for people with Parkinson's, the internal route from intention to movement is broken, that's why they freeze.

External cues provide an alternative route that bypasses the basal ganglia, allowing them to initiate movement.

03 — The gap

The gap in research

Existing research tested the effectiveness of AR cues, it was found to be as effective as physical cues and patients respond well to them.

However, the research was limited to pre-authored routes, which does not allow patients to choose their own destinations.

Existing AR cueing

The route is authored first. The destination is fixed before the session starts and stays fixed. The patient adapts their walking to the plan.

What was missing

An adaptive system that allows patients to choose their own destinations and generates a safe route in real time. In their own home and environement.

The gap in research is user autonomy.

04 — Research

Getting to know my users

I sent a short French survey about walking and freezing to France Parkinson. The manager I reached there has Parkinson's herself. She answered it, then forwarded it through her network. Twelve people replied.

12 responses · France Parkinson network · May–June 2025

Where it happens

Participants were asked to list the triggers that cause freezing. They fell into three categories: terrain, body and mind.

Terrain
  • doorways
  • stairs
  • obstacles
  • kerbs
  • uneven stone
  • narrow passages
Body
  • standing still too long
  • cold
  • fatigue
  • the OFF hours between doses
Mind
  • stress
  • being rushed
  • being watched

Anxiety and terrain are the most common triggers, we can reduce anxiety by giving the user a clear path to follow and avoid terrain that is difficult to navigate.

06 — Fieldwork

Then they invited me to feel it.

France Parkinson runs awareness days where visitors can experience how the disease can affect their daily lives. One of the stations was about freezing of gait, and I was invited to try it out !

It was indeed a difficult short path to walk, I could empathize with the people who have to live with it every day.

07 — The build

Point at where you want to go.

  1. 1
    The headset already knows the room

    Meta's Mixed Reality Utility Kit gives a live mesh of walls, floor and furniture, captured once in the room the person actually walks in.

  2. 2
    You point

    A ray from the controller lands on a spot on the floor. That is the destination. It is yours, and you can change it at any moment — re-targeting redraws the route without erasing the cue under your feet.

  3. 3
    The floor answers

    Unity's NavMesh solves a route around the furniture, spline interpolation takes the jerk out of the corners — a right angle is precisely where freezing happens — and the path is drawn as 15 cm cues at 75 cm spacing, the stride length the cueing literature converges on.

08 — Pilot

Five people, in a room with real furniture in it.

A 5 × 5 m physiotherapy room, furniture as obstacles, one destination chair. Three conditions in order — unassisted, a pre-computed AR path, then the interactive one — so baseline, guidance and adaptivity could be told apart.

    Crossed the room in 10 steps with guidance where he'd taken 26 without — shuffle to full stride. Said knowing where the path went reduced his anxiety about the obstacles, and he froze less.

  • P5 Uses a walker daily

    Gained almost nothing. Her limits were motor, not informational — there was nothing for a cue to unlock.

09 — What happened

All five chose their own target.

Autonomy

Participants walked to destinations they picked, not ones assigned to them. That was the point of the study, and it held for every one of the five.

Learnability

Nobody in the group had used AR before. Pointing at the floor needed one demonstration, and no further explanation.

The cues

Walking improved with the path on. What the survey had already predicted was confirmed in the room: the headset, not the cueing, is what people would refuse.

Two of the five getting little from it isn't a footnote, it's the finding: adaptive cueing is for people whose problem is starting movement rather than producing it, and who can hold a destination in mind while walking to it.

Five participants is a pilot, not a trial. Step counts and observation, not step length, velocity or cadence — the measured gait outcomes are in the ISMAR poster.

10 — Honestly

What I'd change.

The headset is the study's own confound.

A Quest 3 weighs about half a kilo and sits on the face of someone who told me, in writing, that they can't stand anything on their nose. Every result here is measured through that. The next version belongs on light see-through glasses, or it doesn't leave the lab.

Twelve responses is a conversation, not a sample.

It was enough to change what I built. It is not enough to claim anything about the population, and I've tried not to.

Stress is an input the system can't see.

The mesh knows where the doorway is. It doesn't know that someone is watching, or that the person is in an OFF hour. That is the next thing worth reading, and it isn't geometry.

Test it in the hallway, not the study room.

The system was built to work in a person's own home, around their own furniture. So far it has only ever been asked to work in ours.
